Try it

Two tools, built for field researchers, conservationists, educators — and anyone who has ever wondered what that sound was.

The Animal Sound Translator

Upload a clip, record from your microphone, or paste an audio URL. WAVE identifies the species, profiles the acoustics, and cross-references validated research data — reference recordings, species facts, and more. A Compare mode lines up several clips side by side with spectrograms.

Open the Translator How it works

The Sound Map

Add clips from different animals. WAVE builds an acoustic fingerprint for each one and arranges them so that distance means similarity — clips that share spectral structure sit close together. Hover any point to see its spectrogram. This maps acoustic structure, not fabricated meaning.

Open the Sound Map Sound as shape

Where these tools run: the Translator and Sound Map need the live site’s model backend. They work on bioacoustics.cloud today. On the static preview at bioacoustics-cloud.pages.dev the pages load but the model calls have no backend to reach — that preview is for reading the site, not for running predictions.

The Technical Triad

Three layers turn raw sound into structured, comparable meaning.

01

Bio-logging & edge AI

Hydrophones, loggers, and sensors capture signal from dense environmental noise, with inference at the edge — on-device, offline, at remote stations and on marine hydrophones.

02

The Superlet Transform

High-resolution signal processing adapted from neuroscience. It revealed the Chagos pygmy blue whale song is pulsed, not continuous — detail conventional methods blur.

03

Foundation models

NatureLM-audio and Perch 2.0 map vocal patterns without supervision — models trained on one species can help organize another’s. Honest status: these models are wired into the pipeline but not connected yet. Today’s live predictions come from BirdNET.

Straight answers about what WAVE can — and can’t — do

Bioacoustic AI is full of hype. We’d rather earn trust with honest answers.

Can WAVE actually translate what an animal is saying?

Not in the sci-fi sense — and we won’t pretend otherwise. WAVE identifies which species is calling with real confidence scores, profiles the acoustics, and connects each sound to validated research data. Mapping calls to specific meaning is an open scientific frontier; WAVE does not invent sentences. Read the full status report: Can we really translate animals?

What model powers the species prediction?

BirdNET (Cornell Lab of Ornithology + Chemnitz University) — birds-first, over 11,000 species. The production model v2.4 (March 2025) covers 6,522 bird species; the 11,000+ figure is BirdNET's current platform figure. That is the live predictor today. A broader GPU-backed model (Perch 2.0 / NatureLM) for marine mammals and other taxa is wired into the pipeline and activates once a GPU inference endpoint is connected. It is not connected as of November 2026.

Does it work for whales and marine mammals?

Partly. BirdNET alone is not trained on whale song. For marine mammals, WAVE cross-references the Watkins Marine Mammal Sound Database (Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution) — 15k+ annotated clips across 60+ species. Full model prediction for marine taxa waits on the GPU model connection. See Listening to the ocean.

Where does the data come from?

From the public archives listed in the archives section — Xeno-canto, Macaulay Library, Watkins, iNaturalist Sounds, Earth Species Library, Project CETI, the Animal Sound Archive, and GBIF. WAVE links to these collections; it does not own them. See The data landscape of animal translation.

How do I analyze my own recording?

Open the Translator, upload a clip (WAV gives the fullest acoustic profile; MP3 and other formats still cross-reference), record from your microphone, or paste an audio URL. A species hint helps — for example “humpback whale” or “gray wolf”. The tool runs on the live site’s backend.

How accurate is it?

Predictions come with confidence scores, not certainty — and accuracy depends on the species, the recording quality, and how common the target is. A detector that is 99% accurate can still be wrong most of the time when the target is rare. See The base-rate problem: why rare species fool even good detectors.

Is it open source?

The engine is intended to be MIT-licensed and published on GitHub, but the public repository is a stub today — the engine is not published yet. Do not treat “open source” as a live fact until the code lands. Growing the open-source community is the next roadmap step.
