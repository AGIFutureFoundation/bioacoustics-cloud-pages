The Lyrebird's Stolen Songs: Mimicry, Memory, and the Art of Copying

The superb lyrebird copies kookaburras, whipbirds, and chainsaws with uncanny accuracy. Most of what it sings is stolen — and that is the point.

A plain status report from WAVE / bioacoustics.cloud

Illustration: a superb lyrebird male displaying in a damp rainforest, tail fanned over his back. Generated illustration, not a photograph.

Walk into a wet eucalypt forest in southeastern Australia on a winter morning and you may hear something that stops you mid-step: a kookaburra laughing, loud and close, from a patch of ferns at your feet. You look for the bird. There is no kookaburra. There is a grey-brown, pheasant-sized bird scratching in the leaf litter, and it is laughing like a kookaburra, and then, without pausing, it becomes a whipbird — a long, rising whistle ending in a crack — and then a rosella, and then a camera shutter, and then a chainsaw, and then its own song, a liquid cascade of notes that belongs to no other species on Earth.

That bird is a superb lyrebird, Menura novaehollandiae, and it is the greatest mimic in the animal kingdom. Not the most talkative, not the loudest, but the most accomplished at a particular trick that evolution has produced only a handful of times: hearing a sound, remembering it, and reproducing it so accurately that even the animal that made the sound in the first place sometimes cannot tell the copy from the original.

This article is about that trick: how the lyrebird does it, why it does it, what it learns and from whom, where the famous stories about chainsaws and camera shutters end and the exaggeration begins, and what a bird that copies everything it hears means for the science of listening — including the science this site is built on. Because a bird that can imitate a kookaburra is also a bird that can fool a microphone, a classifier, and a researcher, and that is a problem worth understanding properly.

The bird behind the voice

The superb lyrebird is one of two species in the genus Menura and the family Menuridae; the other is the much rarer Albert's lyrebird, found in a small area of rainforest on the Queensland–New South Wales border. The superb lyrebird is endemic to Australia and is found in forest in the southeast of the country, from southern Victoria to southeastern Queensland, in wet sclerophyll forest and temperate rainforest — the damp, fern-filled gullies and dense understorey where sound carries and a ground-dwelling bird can scratch for food in the leaf litter.

It is one of the world's largest songbirds, and the male is unmistakable in display: a huge, ornate tail, fanned up over his back in the shape of a lyre — the ancient Greek harp that gives the bird its name — with two long, curved outer feathers framing a fan of fine, lacy plumes. The display is performed on a mound of earth the male has carefully prepared as a stage, raked clear of leaves, in a patch of forest he defends. He sings from that mound, sometimes for hours, fanning his tail and turning slowly, while females watch from the surrounding ferns.

The female is plainer — no lyre tail, a smaller bird — but she is no less remarkable as a singer. Females sing regularly, producing both lyrebird-specific song and vocal mimicry, and the old idea that only males perform is wrong. Both sexes can intersperse vocal mimicry with lyrebird-specific vocalisations, including songs and alarm calls. The lyrebird's voice is not a male ornament. It is a family trait.

The bird's name in Aboriginal languages includes weringerong, woorail, and bulln-bulln — names that predate European science by thousands of years and that remind us that the lyrebird was known, watched, and named long before it was described scientifically. The superb lyrebird was first illustrated and described scientifically as Menura superba by Major-General Thomas Davies in 1800, in a paper to the Linnean Society of London. It has been famous ever since, and fame has done it no favours: the stories told about it have grown larger than the evidence, and part of this article's job is to separate the two.

The superb lyrebird is currently listed as Least Concern by the IUCN, with stable populations in well-protected national parks and reserves. That is good news, and it is worth stating plainly, because much of the wildlife in this series of articles is in trouble. The lyrebird is not, at present, in trouble — but its habitat is fire-prone forest, and the bushfires that swept southeastern Australia in 2019–2020 burned large areas of lyrebird country, temporarily silencing entire populations. A species can be secure and still be vulnerable to the loss of the forests it depends on. The two facts sit side by side.

The instrument: a voice box like no other

The lyrebird's gift starts with anatomy. A bird's voice comes from the syrinx, the organ at the base of the windpipe where the two bronchi divide — the avian equivalent of a voice box, but one that, unlike the human larynx, has two independently controlled sound sources, one in each bronchus. A bird can, in effect, sing two notes at once.

The lyrebird's syrinx is the most complexly muscled of all the passerines — the songbirds — giving it extraordinary control over the sounds it produces. That musculature is what makes the accuracy possible: the fine control of pitch, rhythm, and amplitude that lets a lyrebird reproduce a kookaburra's laugh or a whipbird's whip with near-perfect fidelity. It is not magic. It is muscle, refined by evolution to a degree no other songbird has matched.

But anatomy is only the beginning. The lyrebird's real gift is learning. Songbirds learn their songs, and the lyrebird learns more, and for longer, than almost any of them. A young lyrebird begins by listening — to adult males, to the birds around it, to the forest itself — and it practises relentlessly, refining its repertoire over years. The quality of mimetic song increases with age, with adult lyrebirds having both greater accuracy and a more diverse repertoire than subadults. A male does not reach his full vocal powers until he is several years old; the learning is a lifetime's work, not a season's.

And what he learns is not only the songs of other birds. The mimicry of the superb lyrebird is highly accurate, with even the model species at times unable to distinguish between model song and mimicked song. An estimated 70–80% of the male's vocalisations consist of imitations of other species — mostly other birds, but occasionally other animals. That figure deserves a pause. Seven or eight sounds out of every ten a male lyrebird makes are copies. His own voice, in the sense of sounds that belong to lyrebirds and no one else, is a minority of what he sings. He is, more than any other bird, a creature made of other creatures' voices.

What a lyrebird copies

The list of sounds a superb lyrebird has been recorded mimicking reads like an inventory of an Australian forest, with a hardware store thrown in.

From other birds: kookaburras, rosellas, whipbirds, currawongs, magpies, butcherbirds, thornbills, treecreepers, honeyeaters — the common voices of the forest, rendered with such fidelity that the model species themselves are sometimes fooled. A lyrebird's imitation of a flock's chatter can sound like a flock. Its imitation of a single bird's alarm call can sound like an alarm.

From other animals: koalas, possums, dingoes — the mammals of the forest, whose voices are lower, rougher, and harder to copy than a bird's, and which the lyrebird copies anyway.

From humans and their machines: a mill whistle, a cross-cut saw, chainsaws, car engines and car alarms, fire alarms, rifle shots, camera shutters, dogs barking, crying babies, music, mobile phone ringtones, and even the human voice. This is the list that made the lyrebird famous, and it is also the list where the stories outran the evidence — a point this article returns to below.

And from its own kind: the lyrebird's own song, a mixture of lyrebird-specific elements and mimicry, delivered in medleys that can last for up to an hour during the breeding season, when males may sing for four hours of the day — almost half the hours of daylight in a southern winter. The breeding season peaks from June to August, the Australian winter, when the forest is cold and damp and the males are singing hardest. That timing is itself a clue: the lyrebird sings when the forest is at its quietest, when its voice carries furthest, and when the females are choosing.

The medleys are not random. A male delivers his imitations in sequences, each item following the last in an order that is itself learned and structured. One male's sequence will differ from another's, because each male has learned from a different set of tutors and a different forest. The repertoire is, in effect, a biography: a record of what a particular bird heard, where, and from whom, over years of listening.

Learning: who teaches a lyrebird to sing

The most interesting question about the lyrebird is not what it copies but how it learns to copy, and the answer turns out to be stranger than the usual story of a young bird learning from its parents.

Juveniles initially learn mimetic items through transmission by older lyrebirds, rather than from the model species themselves. A young lyrebird does not learn the kookaburra's laugh by listening to a kookaburra. It learns it by listening to an older lyrebird imitating a kookaburra. The copy is a copy of a copy, passed down through generations of lyrebirds like a song learned from a recording rather than from a live performance.

That has a striking consequence. A population of superb lyrebirds introduced to Tasmania has retained the calls of species not native to Tasmania in its repertoire, with some local Tasmanian endemic bird songs added. The Tasmanian lyrebirds are singing the voices of mainland birds they have never heard, because their ancestors brought those voices with them, and the voices have been passed down, lyrebird to lyrebird, long after the original models disappeared from earshot. A lyrebird's repertoire is not only a record of its own forest. It is a record of its ancestors' forests, carried across water and across generations.

That makes the lyrebird a living archive — and a cautionary one. If a lyrebird can preserve the call of a bird that no longer lives in its forest, then a recording of a lyrebird is not evidence that the imitated species is present. A microphone in a Tasmanian forest that hears a mainland bird's call may be hearing a lyrebird, not the bird itself. That is a trap for any acoustic survey, and it is one of the reasons the lyrebird matters to bioacoustics beyond its fame: it is the ultimate test case for the question of whether a recording proves a species was there.

The lyrebird also teaches us something about culture. The word is usually reserved for humans, but the lyrebird's song is transmitted socially, varies between populations, and changes over time — the three hallmarks of a cultural tradition. The companion article on dialects and culture takes up that question directly, and the article on dialects in the wild explores how local song traditions form. The lyrebird is the strongest case in the bird world for a tradition that is not genetic but learned, not fixed but changing, and not individual but shared.

Why copy? The sexual selection story

Why would a bird spend years learning to sound like other animals? The leading answer is sexual selection: females prefer males that produce more accurate mimicry and that have a greater diversity of mimetic songs in their repertoire. The mimicry is a display, like the tail, and it is judged by the same audience — the females watching from the ferns.

The logic is the same as the costly signalling logic that shapes the dawn chorus: a display that is hard to produce is a credible signal of quality, because only a bird in good condition, with a good memory, a good ear, and years of practice, can produce it well. A male that can imitate twenty species accurately is advertising his age, his experience, his health, and his learning ability, all at once. A male that can imitate five species badly is advertising the opposite.

That explanation fits the evidence. Adult males have more accurate and more diverse repertoires than subadults. Males sing hardest during the breeding season, when females are choosing. The display mound, the fanned tail, and the medley of imitations are all parts of the same performance, delivered to the same audience, at the same time.

But it is probably not the whole story. Mimicry may also serve in territory defence — a male that sounds like many birds may sound like many birds, and a rival that hears a forest full of voices may think twice about intruding. It may serve in social communication, in alarm, in the sheer pleasure of vocal play. The science has not settled the question, and the article will not pretend otherwise. What is settled is that females prefer accurate, diverse mimicry, and that the preference is strong enough to have driven the evolution of the most complex syrinx in the songbird world.

The chainsaw story: what is true and what is exaggerated

No account of the lyrebird can avoid the chainsaw. The story is famous: a lyrebird in the forest, mimicking the sound of a chainsaw with such accuracy that loggers thought one of their own was working nearby. It has been told in documentaries, in books, in articles — including, in some versions, this one — and it has become the lyrebird's signature anecdote.

Here is the part that is true. Lyrebirds have been recorded mimicking human sounds including chainsaws, cross-cut saws, camera shutters, car alarms, and mobile phone ringtones. A recording of a superb lyrebird mimicking the sounds of an electronic shooting game, workmen, and chainsaws was added to the National Film and Sound Archive's Sounds of Australia registry in 2013 — a formal recognition that the recording is part of Australia's sonic heritage. David Attenborough has featured the superb lyrebird in several documentaries, describing its voice skills as among the most sophisticated in the animal kingdom, and footage of a lyrebird mimicking a camera shutter and a workman's saw in his Life of Birds series brought the bird to a global audience.

Here is the part that is exaggerated. While the mimicry of human noises is widely reported, the extent to which it happens is exaggerated, and the phenomenon is unusual. In fact, this behaviour has to date been documented only in captive specimens, or in birds living close to human activity — not as a routine part of wild lyrebird song. Parts of the lyrebird's own song can resemble human-made sound effects, which has given rise to the urban legend that wild lyrebirds frequently imitate video game or film sounds. They do not, as a rule. The wild lyrebird's repertoire is overwhelmingly the voices of other birds and animals, not the sounds of machines.

That correction matters, because the chainsaw story has done the lyrebird a disservice. It has made the bird famous as a novelty — a feathered tape recorder, a party trick — and it has obscured the more interesting truth: that the lyrebird is a serious subject for the study of vocal learning, memory, culture, and sexual selection, and that its mimicry of other birds is far more accomplished, far more complex, and far more scientifically important than its occasional imitation of a machine. The chainsaw is the least interesting thing a lyrebird can do. The kookaburra is the most interesting, because the kookaburra is the sound the lyrebird has spent millions of years learning to copy, and the chainsaw is a sound it has had a century to notice.

What the lyrebird means for listening machines

The lyrebird is a problem for bioacoustics, and it is a useful problem, because it exposes the limits of every tool in the field.

A classifier trained to identify a kookaburra from its laugh will identify a lyrebird imitating a kookaburra as a kookaburra, unless it has been trained on lyrebird mimicry as well. A survey that counts kookaburras by their calls will over-count them in a forest full of lyrebirds. A researcher who hears a rare bird's call in a recording and reports it as a range extension may be reporting a lyrebird. These are not hypothetical risks. They are the direct consequence of a bird that copies other birds with an accuracy that fools even the model species.

The defences are the same ones that protect acoustic monitoring generally. Train classifiers on mimicry as well as on the model species. Check surprising identifications against reference recordings and, where it matters, by human experts. Combine acoustic data with visual surveys. Treat a single recording as a suggestion, not a verdict. The WAVE article on the base-rate problem explains why rare species fool detectors, and the article on how a species classifier is trained explains what goes into a model and what its blind spots are. The lyrebird is the extreme case of the general problem those articles describe: a world in which sounds are copied, borrowed, and transmitted, and in which a recording is evidence, not proof.

But the lyrebird is also an opportunity. A bird that learns sounds socially, that preserves the calls of absent species, that varies its repertoire by population and by age, is a natural experiment in vocal learning — the same process that underlies human speech, and the same process that the field of animal communication is trying to understand. The companion article on how WAVE decodes animal communication and the article on can we really translate animals take up that larger question. The lyrebird does not answer it. It shows us how hard the question is, and how much there is to learn from a bird that has spent its evolutionary history listening.

How to hear one: a field guide

Hearing a lyrebird is not difficult. Hearing one well takes patience, and it takes place.

The birds live in wet forest in southeastern Australia — the Dandenong Ranges outside Melbourne, the Blue Mountains outside Sydney, Royal National Park, Lamington National Park, and the many smaller reserves and state forests between them. They are ground birds, and they are shy: a lyrebird that hears you coming will slip away through the ferns before you see it, and you will hear it only after it has gone.

The best time is winter, June to August, when the males are singing hardest from their display mounds. Go early, but not necessarily at dawn: lyrebirds sing through the day in winter, and a male on his mound may perform for hours. Find a gully with ferns and leaf litter, where the ground is damp and the canopy is closed. Stand still. Wait. The forest will tell you where the bird is, because the bird will tell you — in the voices of half a dozen other species, all coming from the same patch of ground.

Listen for the sequence. A lyrebird does not sing one imitation and stop. It sings medleys: a kookaburra, then a whipbird, then a rosella, then its own song, each item following the last without a break. That sequencing is the giveaway. A real kookaburra does not follow itself with a whipbird. A lyrebird does, because a lyrebird is performing, not merely calling.

Record it if you can. A phone recording, held still, is enough to capture the medley, and the Macaulay Library and Xeno-canto hold reference recordings you can compare against. Note the date, time, and place. A recording of a lyrebird's repertoire is a record of a local tradition — of which species that population copies, in which order, with what accuracy — and traditions change, which means a recording made this year may not match a recording made in the same forest in ten years' time.

And go back. A lyrebird's repertoire grows with age, and a population's repertoire changes as its tutors die and its young birds learn from new ones. The bird you hear this winter will not sound the same next winter. That is not a flaw in the listening. It is the point: you are hearing a culture, and cultures move.

The limits, stated plainly

The lyrebird is a marvel, and the marvel has limits that deserve to be stated as plainly as its achievements.

First, mimicry is not understanding. There is no evidence that a lyrebird comprehends the meaning of the sounds it copies. A lyrebird that imitates a kookaburra's laugh does not know what the laugh means to a kookaburra, and a lyrebird that imitates an alarm call is not warning anyone. Mimicry serves display and competition, not conversation. The bird is a performer, not a translator, and the distinction matters for anyone tempted to read meaning into the imitation.

Second, the famous human sounds are the exception, not the rule. The wild lyrebird's repertoire is overwhelmingly the voices of other birds and animals. The chainsaw, the camera shutter, and the car alarm are real, but they are rare, and they have been documented mostly in captive birds or birds living close to human activity. A visitor who goes to a forest expecting to hear a chainsaw will be disappointed, and a writer who presents the chainsaw as typical is misleading their reader.

Third, the lyrebird's mimicry is a trap for acoustic monitoring. A recording of a lyrebird can be mistaken for a recording of the species it imitates, and that mistake can propagate into surveys, datasets, and conservation decisions. The defence is care: train classifiers on mimicry, check surprising identifications, keep human experts in the loop, and treat a single recording as a suggestion rather than a verdict.

Fourth, the lyrebird's security is not guaranteed. Least Concern is a status, not a promise. The forests the bird depends on are fire-prone, and the fires of 2019–2020 showed how quickly a population can be silenced. A species that depends on damp gullies and dense understorey is a species that depends on those gullies staying damp and that understorey staying dense, and both are under pressure from a warming climate and from land use. Listening to lyrebirds is not a substitute for protecting the forests they sing in.

Why a bird that copies matters

There is a temptation to treat the lyrebird as a curiosity — a bird that does a trick, a story to tell at dinner. That would be a mistake, and not only because the trick is extraordinary.

The lyrebird matters because it shows us what listening can be. A lyrebird listens the way a scientist listens: carefully, over years, remembering what it heard, comparing it with what it hears now, and building a repertoire from the comparison. It listens the way a culture listens: socially, learning from its elders, preserving what they knew, and adding what it discovers. It listens the way an archive listens: holding the voices of birds that no longer live in its forest, so that they are not lost entirely.

And it matters because it shows us the limits of listening. A lyrebird can copy a sound perfectly and understand none of it. A microphone can record a sound perfectly and identify it wrongly. A model can classify a sound confidently and be fooled by a mimic. Listening, whether by a bird or by a machine, is not the same as understanding, and the gap between the two is where the interesting work lies — for the lyrebird, for the bioacoustician, and for anyone trying to make sense of what animals say to each other.

The lyrebird will be singing this winter in the gullies of southeastern Australia, in the voices of kookaburras and whipbirds and rosellas, and in its own voice underneath them all. The question is whether we will be listening — and whether, having heard it, we will understand what we heard. The bird has spent its evolutionary history learning to listen. We are only beginning.

Glossary

  • Albert's lyrebird: the rarer of the two lyrebird species (Menura alberti), found in a small area of rainforest on the Queensland–New South Wales border.
  • Display mound: a mound of earth a male lyrebird rakes clear of leaves and uses as a stage for his singing and tail display.
  • Mimicry: the reproduction of sounds made by other species — or by machines — with high accuracy.
  • Passerine: a perching bird; a member of the order Passeriformes, the songbirds.
  • Sexual selection: evolution driven by competition for mates, in which a trait spreads because it is preferred by the choosing sex.
  • Spectrogram: a visual representation of sound showing frequency over time; the standard way bioacousticians read a recording.
  • Superb lyrebird: Menura novaehollandiae, the larger and better-known of the two lyrebird species, endemic to southeastern Australia.
  • Syrinx: the avian voice box, located where the windpipe divides, which produces song; the lyrebird's is the most complexly muscled among songbirds.

Frequently asked questions

Can a lyrebird really imitate a chainsaw? But the behaviour is unusual and has been documented mostly in captive birds or birds living close to human activity.
